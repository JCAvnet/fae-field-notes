Does RFSoC design still need a whole team?

RFSoC design asks for RF, FPGA and software skills at once. In my talk at AMD on Wheels New Zealand, AI agents engineered a working radio link on one board. What used to require a team of engineers now requires one.

By Joachim Cain, Field Applications Engineer27 October 20262 min read/Event: AMD on Wheels New Zealand · AUT (Auckland University of Technology), Auckland, New Zealand · 16 October 2026

In my talk “Agentic AI: Enabling RFSoC Design” at AMD on Wheels New Zealand, at Auckland University of Technology (AUT) on 16 October 2026, I opened with a demo, not slides. A message from the room went out as a real radio signal and came back onto the screen. Then I revealed who engineered it: AI agents. What used to require a team of engineers now requires one.

Why RFSoC stays on the “too hard” list

If you’ve looked at a radio frequency system-on-chip (RFSoC), you know the catch:

  • It takes radio frequency (RF), FPGA and software skills at once, so it usually needs a small team.
  • Part of the job sits outside your expertise. For me, that was a custom Linux for the board.
  • The tools can mislead you. A timing report can say PASS while the board on your bench says BROKEN.

One engineer, a set of agents, and the rails

An AMD Zynq UltraScale+ RFSoC puts the data converters, the programmable logic and the processors on one device. From my short, plain-English brief, AI agents wrote the models, the hardware code, the tests and the build scripts, drove AMD Vivado and loaded the result onto my board. The AI does the engineering; you do the judgement.

That helps either way: faster where you know the ground, and it fills the gaps where you don’t. What makes it trustworthy is a harness at each stage: check the plan against AMD’s own intellectual property (IP), build a model first, and put the real board in the loop.

What the demo proved

Each harness caught something real. An agent queried AMD’s IP directly through the Vivado MCP (Model Context Protocol) server and found the obvious plan needed a clock the tile can’t generate. The model caught a “locked” light that was backwards. Only the real board exposed a path needing about 15.9 ns against a 5.5 ns clock, which the timing report had passed. A capture from the board then matched the model bit for bit on nine internal signals across 1023 symbols.

The honest limit: the agents still need me. When my board became unreachable, one stopped and asked me for a power cycle and a decision.
